The whole topic, compressed

No paragraphs to re-read — just the rules, the definitions, and the numbers that show up on exams.

RULE 01
Activators → ortho/para. Deactivators (except halogens) → meta.
RULE 02
Rate is set by the slow step: attack of the electrophile forming the carbonium ion.
RULE 03
More stable carbonium ion → faster substitution.
RULE 04
Charge in the intermediate is strongest ortho and para to the attacked carbon.
RULE 05
Halogens: inductive withdrawal deactivates; resonance donation directs o,p.

Rate-determining step

C₆H₆ + Y⁺ → arenium ion (carbonium ion), slow, rate-determining. A second fast step then loses H⁺ to restore aromaticity.

Faster reaction ⇔ more stable transition state ⇔ more stable carbonium ion (same factors stabilize both).

Why ortho/para & meta happen

An activator raises reactivity at every position but raises ortho/para the most. A deactivator lowers reactivity everywhere but lowers ortho/para the most — so meta becomes the relatively "least bad" option.

Activating groups (release electrons)

Ortho, Para directors
  • —NH₂, —OH, —OCH₃, —NHCOCH₃ (resonance-dominant)
  • —C₆H₅, —CH₃ and other alkyls (inductive)

Deactivating groups (withdraw electrons)

Meta directors
  • —N(CH₃)₃⁺, —NO₂, —CN, —SO₃H
  • —COOH, —CHO, —COR (multiple bond to O/N pulls electrons from the ring)

Halogens — the exception

—X is deactivating (net effect, inductive wins) but ortho, para-directing (resonance wins for orientation, even though it's the weaker of the two effects).

Reactivity ← inductive effect  |  Orientation ← resonance effect

Key numbers to remember

  • tert-Butylbenzene: 16× more reactive than benzene (nitration)
  • Toluene: 25× more reactive than benzene (nitration)
  • Benzyl chloride (CH₂Cl): only ⅓ as reactive as benzene
  • CH₂Cl → CHCl₂ → CCl₃: deactivation gets stronger each step
